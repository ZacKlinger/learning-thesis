## What I read this week

- [Watters 2015 — "Teaching Machines and Turing Machines: The
  History of the Future of Labor and Learning"](../sources/Watters-2015-teaching-machines-and-turing-machines.md) —
  the 10 August 2015 keynote at the Digital Pedagogy Lab Summer
  Institute (UW Madison). Direct precursor to Watters' 2021 book
  *Teaching Machines* (still paywalled and unread). The talk's
  central historical claim is that "labour-saving" ed-tech rhetoric
  is a century old (Thorndike 1912 → Pressey 1933 → Skinner 1950s
  → contemporary "adaptive" and "personalised" learning) — and
  that from Skinner's own 1950s formulation, the "not replacement"
  clause has always come explicitly paired with "he may teach more
  students than heretofore." The productivity gain has historically
  been captured by expanding throughput expectations, not by
  freeing the teacher's time. A secondary argument (using
  essay-grading as the working example) is that the load-bearing
  burden in a substantial part of teaching labour is *affective*,
  not menial — so a labour-saving tool that removes only the
  mechanisable component may leave the exhausting core untouched
  or (worse) amplify it by raising throughput expectations on the
  affective work that remains. Nine primary quotes recorded in the
  source note; three of those (Pressey 1933, Skinner 1950s twice)
  are direct primaries the ledger had previously carried only
  through Cuban 2022's compressed characterisation. This is the
  most focused pre-book Watters read on labour and
  personalisation specifically; not a substitute for the 2021 book,
  and not a substitute for Watters 2018 (contemporary-decade) or
  Watters 2019 (decade-in-review).

## Tensions I noticed

The most interesting tension this week is between **the Watters
2015 affective-labour argument** and **the Cuban 2025 teacher-voice
data on which the workload-as-condition reframing has been leaning
most heavily.**

Watters's argument: the exhausting core of teaching is *affective*
(the emotional and intellectual cost of caring about a specific
student's writing, sitting with a specific student's misconception,
being available for a specific student's crisis). It is not
mechanisable, and any labour-saving tool that removes only the
mechanisable component may leave the exhausting core untouched or
amplify it. She reads bottom-up AI adoption suspiciously — the
Ballaret / Wallace / Roberts pattern, on her framing, is not
teachers reclaiming judgement but a performance of care that
extracts affective attachment.

Cuban 2025's data reads the same phenomenon oppositely: Ballaret
reclaiming his summer, Wallace using AI to help with parent
communication, Roberts using AI on per-student feedback are, in
Cuban's presentation, teachers exercising judgement about which
tasks the tool is good at and which it isn't. The pattern is
teacher-directed, bottom-up, and (by Cuban's implicit inference)
distinct from the top-down deployments Watters is critiquing.

These are not two accounts of two different phenomena; they are two
accounts of the same phenomenon. Both cannot be right as summary
characterisations. The essay owes a position on which reading is
correct for the bulk of the pattern — or, alternatively, a
mechanism by which they are both partially right (e.g., the
individual teacher's experience is what Cuban describes, and the
system-level response Watters warns about will land on that
individual gain and metabolise it).

The Roberts case in Cuban 2025 is the specific test-case that
sharpens the disagreement: Roberts explicitly says "I do not look
at the feedback it gives every single student" — that is affective
labour explicitly offloaded. Watters would read this as the
substitution the steer warns against; Cuban presents it neutrally
in a list of use-cases. The teacher-agency thread now carries this
tension explicitly, with the affective-vs-menial distinction as the
frame inside which the disagreement plays out.

A secondary tension: the **Skinner-form bargain claim** (new this
Saturday on the tech-cycles thread) and the **2026-05-27 steer's
constraint #3** ("the system-imposed workload that forces teachers
to teach to the middle of 30") are not in direct contradiction, but
they are in productive tension. The steer's constraint #3 asserts
that AI's role is to give teachers back the time and
personalisation-bandwidth the system has been taking from them.
Watters 2015's Skinner primary shows that the historical form of
that promise has consistently been redirected into throughput
expansion — teachers freed from "mechanisable functions" have
consistently ended up expected to teach more students, not to have
more time. The steer's claim can be defended (Cuban 2025 is a
first-wedge data point for a possibly-different bargain in the AI
case), but the defence has to be specific. Simply asserting that
AI removes constraint #3 without engaging the Skinner-form bargain
does not answer Watters's historical evidence.

## Thesis-fit check

Operative direction (2026-05-27 steer, constraint #3): *"The
system-imposed workload that forces teachers to teach to the middle
of 30. This is a constraint on the *system*, not a deficiency in
teachers. AI's role here is to give teachers back the time and
personalization-bandwidth the system has been taking from them."*

**This week's reading complicates the steer's constraint #3 in a
specific way.** The historical form of the labour-saving bargain in
schooling has been "not replacement, but larger classes." The 2026
AI case is not automatically outside that pattern; the argument that
it is has to be made explicitly, not assumed.

Two candidate defences on the ledger:

*(1) The bottom-up-adoption datum.* Cuban 2025 shows that the current
adoption pattern is teacher-initiated (bottom-up), not district-
procured (top-down). The Skinner-form bargain historically has been a
system-level response to a system-level deployment; a bottom-up
individual-teacher adoption pattern may not be subject to the same
system-level response. This is a *possibly-different bargain* claim,
not a *certainly-different bargain* claim, and it has not had time to
encounter the system-level response.

*(2) The tool-affordance datum.* Watters 2015 does not distinguish
carefully between mechanised drill (Pressey/Skinner's original tool)
and general-purpose conversational tools (2026's tool). The Baker 1993
aim-shapes-tool finding on the ACOT ledger shows that ambitious aims
did produce different tool-selection at ACOT (enrichment →
problem-solving software; basic-skills → drill). If a general-purpose
LLM is less method-carrying than 1990-era software, the aim-shapes-
tool mechanism operates more strongly than at ACOT — but so does the
Cuban 2022 warning about the tool defaulting to
programmed-learning-at-fidelity in the absence of a specific ambitious
aim. This cuts both ways for the steer's constraint #3 defence.

Neither defence is closed. The steer's positive case remains
defensible; the specific challenge Watters 2015 puts to it needs an
explicit answer in the essay — recorded as a claim on the tech-cycles
thread this Saturday.
