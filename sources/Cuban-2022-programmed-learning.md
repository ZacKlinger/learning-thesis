## Thesis

A compact history-of-a-concept post. Cuban traces "programmed
learning" — the Skinner-tradition programme of pre-test, step-by-step
content presentation, and immediate feedback on right/wrong — from
Pressey's 1928 teaching machine, through its 1950s–60s "huge splash,"
to its disappearance from reform rhetoric by the 1970s, to its
resurrection under the "computer-assisted instruction" (CAI) label in
the 1990s, to its present-day survival in two forms: (a) drill-and-
practice software, and (b) the ubiquitous classroom worksheet — though
Cuban notes that the worksheet version has lost the immediate-feedback
element that was the theoretical core of the programme. The tradition,
Cuban says, "has zig-zagged through schools for over 70 years."

The load-bearing move for the thesis is the continuity claim: the
*theories* underlying programmed learning "remain alive and well in
the software that many schools and individual teachers use for
lessons." What changed was the vocabulary, not the underlying design
of instruction. The rhetoric moved from "programmed learning" (1960s)
to "computer-assisted instruction" (1990s) to "competency-based
education" and "individualized" / "personalized" instruction (1970s
onward and continuing) — but the atomised-steps-with-feedback structure
is preserved across those relabelings.

For the tech-cycles thread the post is a mild version of the
recurrence claim Watters 2019 makes in a sharper mode. Cuban's tone is
diagnostic rather than condemnatory: the tradition did not "utterly
fail" (Watters' 2019 closing sentence); it was absorbed, renamed, and
partially preserved in a lower-fidelity form. The two readings are
compatible on the underlying pattern (the tradition persists across
renames) and diverge on the evaluation (Watters: this is failure
recycling; Cuban: this is how ed-tech traditions get metabolised).

For the augmentation-not-efficiency thread the post is the *efficiency*
side's ancestor line documented at its own root. Programmed learning
is the paradigm case of the efficiency framing — reduce learning to
atomised steps with feedback, deliver via machine. Its persistence in
worksheets is Cuban's claim that this design theory is not a fringe
tradition but the modal one for teacher-generated drill practice at
K-12 scale. This is important for how the augmentation thread reads
the AI-tutoring case: the *default* design pattern LLMs will be
plugged into (worksheet-plus-immediate-feedback) is a lineage
descendant of Pressey/Skinner, not of Scardamalia & Bereiter.

For the teacher-agency thread the post is a small but real datum: the
worksheet is a teacher-authored version of programmed learning, and
its ubiquity is teacher-adopted, not top-down imposed. This is a
subtle counter to the framing that programmed-learning-style ed-tech
is always something *done to* teachers. Some of it is teachers doing
it to themselves as a workload-management strategy — which reframes
the question of what teachers would do differently if the workload
constraint were removed.

- (Opening summary of the cycle.) "'programmed learning' (or
  'programmed instruction') made a huge splash in media during the
  1950s and 1960s. It was an innovation that grabbed the attention
  of policymakers and tech-driven school reformers. Yet by the 1970s,
  it had largely disappeared from the rhetoric of school reformers.
  Then in the 1990s with the installation of computer labs and
  widespread student access to these devices, policymakers and tech-
  enthused school reformers resurrected 'programmed learning.'"
  — line 256
- (The tradition-persists claim.) "Although programmed learning goes
  by different names, the theories that drove it decades earlier
  remain alive and well in the software that many schools and
  individual teachers use for lessons. Here, then, is a curricular
  and instructional innovation that has zig-zagged through schools
  for over 70 years."
  — line 260
- (Definition of the core mechanism.) "The learning program is
  broken down into slivers of knowledge or steps. After users
  complete each step, they are given questions to test their
  comprehension. After entering answers, students are then
  immediately given the correct answer on the screen."
  — line 268
- (Worksheets as descendants — with the feedback element lost.)
  "Teacher-made versions of programmed learning have become
  ubiquitous in the worksheets that elementary and secondary
  classroom teachers use weekly across academic subjects."
  — line 280
- (The degradation claim.) "Most worksheets, however, lack the
  immediate feedback to students that programmed learning promised.
  ... That separation in time between students answering questions
  and finding out which answers were incorrect undermines the
  immediate feedback promised in the theory and practice of
  programmed learning."
  — line 284
- (Absorption into "personalization" rhetoric.) "By the 1970s and
  1980s, programmed learning had become part of the kit bag of
  techniques that teachers could use in other highly hyped school
  reforms aimed at individualizing (or 'personalizing') instruction.
  For example, within the hype surrounding 'competency based
  education' (CBE) and 'computer-assisted instruction' (CAI) rested
  the guided drill and practice worksheets that earlier generations
  of teachers had students complete."
  — line 308
- (Pressey origin footnote.) "In 1928, psychologist Sidney Pressey
  created the first teaching machine using programmed learning. By
  the 1960s, the dream of 'teaching machines' had morphed into
  computer-assisted instruction."
  — line 324

## Where this sits in the canon

- The tech-cycles thread's seventh Cuban/Watters entry. Sits next to
  Cuban 2021 (ACOT retrospective) as the second *specific historical
  case* Cuban has documented on the ledger — 2021 handles the
  1985–1995 hardware-first case, 2022 handles the 1928-onward
  instructional-model case. Together they trace two distinct
  historical strands of the tech-in-schools tradition.
- The Skinner/Pressey lineage anchor the tech-cycles canon has been
  waiting for. Watters' 2018/2019 pieces reference Skinner in
  passing but do not develop the historical arc; Cuban 2022 is a
  compressed version of the arc from a practitioner-historian
  register. Reading Watters (2021) *Teaching Machines* — still
  paywalled — is the next step to have the full historical
  treatment on the ledger.
- Natural pair to the Scardamalia & Bereiter 1994 "third way"
  framing already on the augmentation thread. S&B explicitly
  rejected the drill-and-practice / programmed-learning paradigm
  as the model for computer use in schools; Cuban 2022 documents
  that the paradigm S&B rejected never went away, it just got
  renamed. This makes S&B's "third way" claim more historically
  specific — the "first way" and "second way" S&B were arguing
  against are the same lineage Cuban 2022 traces.
- Complements Watters 2019 rather than substituting for it. Watters'
  mode is chronicle-and-condemnation; Cuban 2022's mode is
  diagnostic-and-mild. On the same historical record they arrive at
  compatible descriptions but incompatible evaluations. The essay
  will have to decide which register it inherits — or find a third.

## Tensions / contradictions

- **vs. Watters 2019's "utter failure" reading.** Cuban 2022 does
  not read the Pressey-Skinner-CAI-personalization arc as failure.
  He reads it as a tradition that survived by renaming and
  degradation — the software preserves the theory, the worksheets
  preserve the practice without the feedback. This is a *mild*
  version of Watters' recurrence claim: the tradition persists,
  but as evolution (with fidelity loss), not as failure recycling.
  The augmentation thread's rhetorical-proximity warning applies
  to Cuban's post too, but at reduced amplitude — Cuban's tone is
  not "beware of another wave of ed-tech hype" but "here is what
  happens to ed-tech traditions when they stop being new."
- **vs. the augmentation-not-efficiency thread's positive case.**
  The persistence of programmed-learning-descendant patterns in
  worksheets and drill software is a constraint on the augmentation
  thread's "AI enables new kinds of learning" claim: the *default*
  design pattern LLMs will be plugged into by most teachers is the
  worksheet-with-immediate-feedback pattern, which is the Pressey
  tradition at full strength (or at higher strength than the
  paper-worksheet version, because feedback is now recovered). AI
  in the modal deployment might not be "the augmentation aim" — it
  might be "programmed learning finally with feedback again." The
  augmentation thread's positive case has to specify what
  distinguishes an AI-tutor deployment that is augmentation from
  an AI-tutor deployment that is programmed learning at fidelity.
- **vs. Cuban 2021's ACOT case.** The two posts are complementary
  but they document different failure modes. ACOT (Cuban 2021) is a
  *hardware-scale* case where the ambition was 1:1 devices; the
  instructional model was left implicit and the surrounding school
  conditions swallowed the innovation. Programmed learning (Cuban
  2022) is an *instructional-model-scale* case where the ambition
  was a specific theory of learning; the theory persisted but the
  practice was implemented at degraded fidelity in worksheet form.
  The two failure modes are distinct and both are on the historical
  ledger the tech-cycles thread has to explain.
- **The bottom-up-adoption caveat.** Cuban 2022 is the first entry
  on the ledger that documents *teacher adoption* of a programmed-
  learning-descendant tradition (the worksheet) at scale, on
  teacher initiative, over decades. This complicates the Cuban 2025
  bottom-up-adoption datum: bottom-up adoption is not new, and it
  is not by itself evidence that a tradition is producing ambitious
  learning. The worksheet-adoption case is the closest historical
  comparator to the Cuban 2025 teacher-AI-adoption case, and it is
  a case where bottom-up adoption preserved a low-fidelity form of
  the original theory.
