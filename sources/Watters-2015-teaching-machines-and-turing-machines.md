## Thesis

Watters' 10 August 2015 keynote at the Digital Pedagogy Lab Summer
Institute (UW Madison) makes a compact argument: the twentieth-century
history of "teaching machines" is a history of ideological pressure to
render teaching and learning more *efficient* — and the "efficiency"
frame is the language of business, not of pedagogy. Thorndike's 1912
programmed-book description, Edison's 1913/1922 predictions about
motion pictures replacing textbooks, Pressey's 1933 call for an
"industrial revolution in education," and Skinner's 1950s teaching-
machines writings all sit on the same century-long lineage as
contemporary "adaptive learning" and "personalization." The specific
insight Watters develops that Cuban 2022 does not: the "not-
replacement" claim is itself an old rhetorical move — Pressey and
Skinner explicitly promised *not* to replace teachers, only to free
them from "drudgery" and grow class size / student-throughput. The
lineage Watters draws includes: Turing's 1950 imitation-game gender
premise; ELIZA (Weizenbaum, mid-1960s) as the earliest cautionary
tale about people forming affective attachments to systems that
demonstrably did not care; the feminisation and affective character
of teaching labour that "efficiency" framings systematically fail to
account for; and the contemporary "personalisation" agenda as
Thorndike-1912 finally becoming operational. The talk is the direct
predecessor to Watters' 2021 book *Teaching Machines*.

The talk is *not* peer-reviewed empirical work. It is a
practitioner-critic / historian of ed-tech in polemical form. Its
evidentiary weight is (a) as a first-hand read of Watters that
puts specific primary quotes from Thorndike, Edison, Pressey, and
Skinner on the ledger, and (b) as a historical framing that
sharpens the specific challenge the 2026-05-27 steer's
"method-agnostic in service of the aim" line has to answer: the
"free teachers from drudgery so they can do the ambitious thing"
argument is not new. It is the same argument Pressey made in 1933
and Skinner made in the 1950s, and — Watters argues — the
historical record of what happened after that promise is a record
of teachers doing *more* work under the banner of
mechanisation, not less.

## Key quoted claims

- (On the century-old lineage of teaching-machines rhetoric.)
  "People have been working to replace, streamline, render more
  efficient education labor with machines for a century." — Watters
  2015 (talk transcript; no page numbering; text ¶25 counting the
  first paragraph after the "delivered today at..." dateline as ¶1).

- (Watters quoting Thorndike, 1912 — the original programmed-book
  claim, framed as the historical seed of contemporary
  "personalisation.")  "If, by a miracle of mechanical ingenuity, a
  book could be so arranged that only to him who had done what was
  directed on page one would page two become visible, and so on,
  much that now requires personal instruction could be managed by
  print." — Thorndike 1912, quoted at text ¶4 and re-quoted at ¶75
  as the "original promise" of personalisation.

- (Watters quoting Edison, ca. 1922, on motion pictures — the most-
  cited historical instance of the tech-transforms-schools hype
  pattern.)  "I believe that the motion picture is destined to
  revolutionize our educational system and that in a few years it
  will supplant largely, if not entirely, the use of textbooks."
  — Edison, in text ¶6. Companion in the same paragraph: Edison's
  efficiency claim that motion picture will yield "one hundred
  percent efficiency" where schoolbooks yield "about two percent."

- (Watters quoting Sidney Pressey, 1933 — the ancestor of the
  contemporary "AI frees teachers from drudgery" argument, made
  ninety years before AI.)  "There must be an 'industrial
  revolution' in education, in which educational science and the
  ingenuity of educational technology combine to modernize the
  grossly inefficient and clumsy procedures of conventional
  education... There will be many labor-saving schemes and devices,
  and even machines – not at all for the mechanizing of education,
  but for the freeing of teacher and pupil from educational drudgery
  and incompetence." — Pressey 1933, quoted at text ¶24.

- (Watters quoting Skinner, 1950s — the "not replacement, freed
  for higher work" trope Watters puts on the century-old list.)
  "Will machines replace teachers? On the contrary, they are capital
  equipment to be used by teachers to save time and labor. In
  assigning certain mechanizable functions to machines, the teacher
  emerges in his proper role as an indispensable human being. He may
  teach more students than heretofore – that is probably inevitable
  if the worldwide demand for education is to be satisfied – but he
  will do so in fewer hours and with fewer burdensome chores. In
  return for his greater productivity he can ask society to improve
  his economic condition." — Skinner, 1950s, quoted at text ¶27.

- (Watters' own diagnostic gloss on the Skinner quote — the two-part
  bargain the "not-replacement" claim historically came with.)
  "Greater productivity. Larger classes. Global demand met through
  mechanization. Sound familiar?" — Watters at text ¶28.

- (On the affective character of teaching labour, which "efficiency"
  framings cannot see.)  "Grading essays isn't necessarily
  burdensome because it's menial, for example; grading essays is
  burdensome because it is affective labor; it is emotionally and
  intellectually exhausting." — Watters at text ¶49.

- (On ELIZA and the deep affective attachment users form with
  systems that demonstrably do not care.)  "Weizenbaum's students
  knew the program did not actually care. Yet they still were eager
  to chat with it and to divulge personal information to it." —
  Watters at text ¶57.

- (On the "personalisation" agenda as the operational realisation of
  Thorndike 1912.)  "Personalization. Automation. Management. The
  algorithms will be crafted, based on our data, ostensibly to suit
  us individually, more likely to suit power structures in turn that
  are increasingly opaque." — Watters at text ¶76.

## Where this sits in the canon

- **The direct precursor to Watters (2021)** *Teaching Machines: The
  History of Personalized Learning* — the book that is on the
  wishlist as paywalled. This 2015 talk is a compact version of that
  book's central argument. The book is the fuller version but has
  not been readable; the talk is what is on the ledger.
- **A first-hand Watters read of the Skinner primary** that the
  ledger has previously carried only through Cuban 2022's
  "programmed learning goes by different names" claim. Watters
  quotes Skinner's actual 1950s formulation of the
  "not-replacement" bargain. Reading Skinner through Watters (as
  a critical historian) is different from reading Skinner through
  Cuban (as a diagnostic historian) — Watters foregrounds the
  larger-classes clause; Cuban's 2022 focus is on
  degradation-in-transmission over decades. Both readings are on
  the ledger now; Skinner directly is still on the wishlist as
  the Wayback retries have not delivered.
- **Not a substitute for Watters (2018).** Watters 2018 is the
  contemporary-decade critique; Watters 2015 is the century-scale
  historical framing. The two do different work. This talk is
  three years earlier and more focused on the labour question
  specifically — automation, gendered/feminised affective
  labour, and the "personalisation" pattern from Thorndike
  forward.
- **Not a substitute for Cuban 2022.** Watters 2015 covers the same
  Pressey/Skinner lineage Cuban 2022 covers but from a different
  analytical position: Watters (critical / labour-focused) reads
  the lineage as a century of anti-labour ideological work
  disguised as pedagogical progress; Cuban (diagnostic /
  historian-of-practice) reads the same lineage as an
  absorption-with-degradation pattern where the theoretical core
  (immediate feedback in Pressey's design) got lost as the
  practice degraded into worksheets. The paired reading
  triangulates the Pressey/Skinner lineage from two
  practitioner-historian perspectives per queue.md item #2.

## Tensions / contradictions

- **With the 2026-05-27 steer's "AI removes constraint #3
  (system-imposed workload)" formulation.** Watters 2015's central
  historical claim is that "labor-saving devices" in ed-tech have
  historically *not* saved labour — they have expanded the throughput
  expectation. Skinner's own words: teachers freed from mechanisable
  functions "may teach more students than heretofore — that is
  probably inevitable if the worldwide demand for education is to be
  satisfied." The Baker 1993 ACOT finding that ACOT teachers reported
  "fairly typical" practice with only "occasional experimentation"
  after three years of extensive computer exposure sits inside this
  pattern. Any 2026 case for AI-removes-workload owes a specific
  argument for what is structurally different about the current
  bargain — or an explicit accounting of the historical bargain and
  why it will not repeat.
- **With the augmentation-thread positive case.** Watters puts
  Skinner's "not replacement, capital equipment" formulation on the
  century-old list of anti-labour ideological moves. That is the
  exact vocabulary the augmentation-not-efficiency thread carries
  in its 2018-annotated status paragraph. The thread now has two
  independent challenges to its own vocabulary: Watters 2018 called
  "augment, not replace" a century-old corporate marketing trope
  (in aggregate); Watters 2015 gives the primary-source instance
  (Skinner 1950s) that started the specific "not replacement, freed
  for higher work" version of that trope. The augmentation thread's
  design-position claim owes a specific answer to how the current
  moment escapes the Skinner-form bargain.
- **With Cuban 2024/2025 (contemporary AI voices).** Watters 2015's
  labour-critical position is at maximum tension with Cuban 2025's
  curated teacher voices, several of whom (Ballaret in particular)
  report genuine workload-relief. Two readings compatible with both
  sources are: (a) the individual teacher's experience of
  workload-relief is real and the system-level pattern is still
  Skinner-form (teachers who reclaim summer time end up expected
  to do more during the year, or the sector-scale response is to
  raise per-teacher load to match the new tool); (b) the current
  moment is genuinely different because the tool is
  teacher-directed rather than management-directed (Cuban 2025's
  point), and the Skinner-form bargain does not automatically
  bind. Neither reading is falsified by either source.
- **Internal to Watters 2015.** The talk's affective-labour
  argument is compatible with two positions the thesis has not
  had to choose between: (i) affective labour is intrinsic to
  what teaching *is*, so any technology that automates it removes
  the very thing being replaced; (ii) affective labour is
  contingent to the current organisational form of schooling, and
  the question is whether the technology's role in the classroom
  preserves or removes it. Watters clearly holds (i). The thesis's
  augmentation-aim framing is closer to (ii). The disagreement is
  substantive and load-bearing for the essay.
